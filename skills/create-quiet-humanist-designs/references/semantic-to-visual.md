# Semantic-to-Visual Translation

Use this reference before choosing style, palette, composition, or image-generation prompts.

## 1. Separate names from meaning

A title often names a feature without explaining it. Expand the content into:

| Layer | Question |
|---|---|
| Entity | What is the product, subject, event, or story? |
| Mechanism | What does it actually do? |
| Input | What material, information, behavior, or situation enters? |
| Action | What is examined, shaped, compared, moved, revealed, repaired, or created? |
| Output | What comes out or becomes visible? |
| Evidence | What trace proves the change? |
| User | Who acts, observes, decides, or benefits? |
| Stakes | Why does the result matter? |

Do not illustrate the feature name until the mechanism is understood.

## 2. Build a semantic field

Generate candidate imagery across these roles:

- **Direct subject:** the actual person, object, place, interface, document, instrument, material, or environment involved.
- **Tool:** the thing that performs or enables the action; prefer a mechanism-specific tool over a universal technology icon.
- **Gesture:** a hand, body, cursor, eye, or physical act showing how a person engages.
- **Trace:** marks, revisions, shadows, layers, fragments, stains, paths, scores, annotations, differences, or residues.
- **Transformation:** the visible before-and-after relationship.
- **Environment:** the desk, room, workshop, archive, street, stage, page, machine, or landscape where the idea lives.
- **Analogy:** a physical system with the same logic; use only when the shared mechanism is precise.

## 3. Create an anchor inventory

Write 6–10 candidates:

| Candidate | Semantic role | Specific content link | Possible action | Visible trace | Familiarity risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [object] | [tool/trace/etc.] | [why it belongs] | [verb] | [result] | [low/medium/high] |

Do not shortlist an anchor merely because it is easy to generate.

## 4. Score candidates

Score 1–5:

- **specificity:** belongs to this content rather than many unrelated topics;
- **recognition:** readable at a glance;
- **agency:** can visibly do or undergo something;
- **trace:** can create evidence or consequence;
- **composition:** has a strong silhouette, structure, or spatial possibility;
- **freshness:** avoids recent outputs and category clichés;
- **emotional fit:** supports the intended tone.

Discard candidates with specificity below 3 unless paired with a highly specific supporting clue.

## 5. Form a visual sentence

Use:

> A [primary anchor] [specific action] with/on/through [supporting clue], causing [visible trace], which makes [content truth] visible.

Strong:

> A revision pencil walks across a stack of successive drafts, and each page becomes less rigid while its meaning-bearing lines remain pinned.

Weak:

> Abstract lines represent improvement.

Strong:

> A reader holds one translucent sentence sheet over another; suspicious repetitions align into a visible pattern in the overlap.

Weak:

> A magnifying glass represents detection.

The object alone is not the concept. The relationship between object, action, and trace is the concept.

## 6. Use literalness deliberately

Choose a level:

1. **Documentary:** show the real user, place, or workflow.
2. **Concrete editorial:** draw, print, collage, or stage a recognizable object or action with one precise unexpected relationship.
3. **Mechanistic analogy:** use a physical system that shares the same process.
4. **Symbolic:** use a culturally legible sign with specific supporting evidence.
5. **Abstract:** use form, rhythm, or material alone.

For product features, promotional posters, social graphics, and title visuals, default to level 2 with hand-drawn, printed, collaged, or constructed treatment. Use level 1 when the real person or workflow is the message. Use level 3 when the mechanism can be represented accurately. Use level 4 carefully. Use level 5 only when the subject or user explicitly calls for abstraction.

Do not mistake realism for clarity. A simplified ink drawing of a specific tool in action may communicate better than a photoreal scene.

## 7. Build a concrete symbolic protagonist when agency is central

Use this route when the content involves agreement, refusal, persuasion,
authority, assistance, authorship, conflict, care, performance, or another
behavior that a viewer can recognize in an actor.

Derive the protagonist from five parts:

| Part | Question |
|---|---|
| Role | What force, habit, person, or system is acting? |
| Silhouette | What physical structure makes it recognizable at thumbnail size? |
| Prop | What content-specific tool or object proves where it belongs? |
| Behavior | What repeated gesture or action reveals its character? |
| Trace | What changes, accumulates, breaks, branches, stains, or remains afterward? |

Write a temporary descriptive name from role plus behavior, such as “the
nodding assistant” or “the editor who erases every pause.” A strong name helps
the design team remember the visual logic. It is not permission to reuse the
character in unrelated work.

The character must do more than pose. Its body, prop, gesture, and consequence
should form the visual sentence. Compare it with at least one object,
environment, process, or trace route before selecting it.

Do not force personification when the content is better explained by a real
workflow, document, material transformation, or physical mechanism.

## 8. Branch without canonical mappings

For every brief, create at least three semantic routes from different roles: direct subject, action, trace, environment, transformation, or precise analogy.

Do not store topic-to-object pairs in the skill. Do not reuse a prior anchor because it illustrated a neighboring topic. Compare current candidates on specificity, recognition, agency, trace, freshness, and emotional fit.

Examples supplied by a user are evidence about reasoning quality, not default visual assets.

## 9. Recognition tests

### Headline-off test

Hide the title. Ask what subject area a viewer would guess, what action is happening, and what changed or was discovered. If none can be answered, strengthen the concrete clues.

### Substitution test

Replace the main anchor mentally with a shoe, plant, or unrelated object. If the concept still works unchanged, it is too generic.

### Category-neighbor test

Compare with adjacent topics. A writing-analysis image should not read equally well as cybersecurity, finance, or meditation.

### Thumbnail test

Reduce to feed size. The anchor and action should remain visible before texture and small copy.

### Explanation test

If the visual requires more than one short sentence to connect it to the content, simplify the relationship.

### Nameable-protagonist test

When a character route exists, hide the title and describe the figure in five
words or fewer. If the best description is only “person,” “robot,” “writer,”
or “cute paper figure,” strengthen the role, silhouette, prop, behavior, or
trace.

### Portrait-without-action test

Freeze or remove the protagonist's action. If the meaning does not weaken, the
character is probably decorative. Give it a more specific gesture,
interaction, interruption, or consequence.

## 10. Avoid semantic clichés without banning objects

Do not maintain a universal blacklist. A familiar object can work when:

- the content genuinely involves it;
- its behavior is specific;
- it combines with a non-generic clue;
- it has not been overused in the series;
- another candidate did not communicate better.

Reject reflexive use, not the object itself.
