# Artistic Editorial Language

## Core distinction

An artistic editorial design is not a polished poster with a rough texture applied. Its subject, spacing, line, color, and type share one authored logic.

Protect meaning, then permit one part of the visual system to behave unexpectedly:

- an image island is much smaller than the field;
- a familiar object is drawn at an awkward scale;
- type wanders through the image rather than filling a rectangle;
- a flat color block interrupts an otherwise sparse drawing;
- a diagram becomes poetic through spacing and material;
- a line continues beyond the object and becomes composition.

Choose one or two such moves. Do not make every element eccentric.

## Mark language

Prefer marks that look selected rather than simulated:

- thin black contour that varies through pressure and hesitation;
- wax-crayon or dry-brush fills that do not close perfectly at edges;
- dense black print used as one heavy anchor;
- flat pigment with visible roller, stamp, or screen behavior;
- halftone reserved for one material or depth layer;
- a corrected line left visible beside the final line.

Do not make every contour wobble. A naive line can be sparse, confident, and structurally precise.

## Color behavior

Build palettes by proportion, not by listing tasteful colors.

Default structure:

- 55–85% paper, white, pale tint, or one quiet field;
- 10–30% one dominant imperfect pigment;
- 3–15% a second pigment;
- 0–5% a signal dot, line, or correction color;
- black or dark graphite as structure rather than a background wash.

Useful relationships include:

- warm paper + carbon + electric blue + old red + one yellow signal;
- white field + dusty pink + olive + rust;
- acid yellow field + faded blue line;
- peach field + black contour + paper white;
- cool white + green display type + black drawing.

Do not canonize these combinations. Change hue and proportion from the content and references. Reject default “muted mustard + teal + cream” when it functions only as instant vintage styling.

## Space and scale

Treat empty space as a shape with pressure.

Use:

- one compact drawing island inside a much larger field;
- fragments separated by pauses rather than aligned to a grid;
- a large ordinary object cropped by an edge;
- a tiny factual phrase far from the focal drawing;
- an off-center cluster that feels balanced by color, not symmetry;
- an object pair whose scale mismatch creates the story.

Avoid:

- automatic 40/60 copy-and-image splits;
- equal margins around every component;
- centered hero-object composition;
- a clean headline column placed beside a completed illustration;
- filling empty areas because they appear unfinished.

## Image–type integration

Exact wording still requires deterministic typesetting, but deterministic type need not look corporate.

Use type as:

- an annotation beside a drawn arrow;
- a repeated rhythm around small image islands;
- a hand-cut or hand-set headline whose letter spacing varies deliberately;
- a small phrase embedded inside a color patch;
- a line that completes, labels, or contradicts the drawing;
- a title that changes scale across words.

Keep factual copy readable. Apply irregularity to display type, short annotations, and spacing—not to URLs, dates, prices, data, or accessibility-critical text.

Do not default to condensed uppercase plus monospaced support copy. Select type behavior from the reference grammar and concept.

## Associative diagrams

A process does not need a corporate flowchart. It may become an editorial diagram when:

- input, change, and result remain accurate;
- arrows, dots, fragments, or repeated objects carry real sequence;
- one concrete object anchors the association;
- spacing creates rhythm without breaking order;
- labels are sparse and exact.

Avoid decorative nodes, generic icons, and machinery added only to make an idea look technical.

## Controlled naivety

Naive is a deliberate economy, not incompetence.

Allow:

- simplified anatomy with believable contact;
- incomplete perspective when silhouette and action remain clear;
- uneven lettering with readable words;
- oversized or undersized objects with a semantic reason;
- crayon blocks that cross a contour;
- one charmingly literal clue.

Reject:

- broken hands and impossible joints;
- generic childish doodles;
- random suns, flowers, stars, or faces unrelated to the brief;
- fake handwritten paragraphs;
- using “cute” to avoid solving the concept.

## Ordinary-poster rejection

Reject a direction before production when it relies on three or more of these:

- one large sans-serif headline in a safe corner;
- one attractive realistic object;
- one empty copy column;
- a tasteful complementary palette;
- a subtle paper texture over everything;
- a literal before/after scene;
- balanced professional spacing;
- a support line in generic typewriter styling;
- no visual decision that would feel risky without the reference board.

Replace at least two structural choices, not only the palette.

## Direction checklist

For each direction, state:

1. What remains immediately understandable?
2. What is the single authored disruption?
3. What is the dominant field and its percentage?
4. Which two or three mark types are used?
5. How does type touch, interrupt, label, or become part of the image?
6. Which ordinary-poster habit is forbidden?
7. What still works at thumbnail size?
